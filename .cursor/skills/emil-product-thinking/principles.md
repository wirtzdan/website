# The twenty principles, in full

Each principle: the claim, the reasoning, the concrete story it comes from, the trap it guards against, and the questions to ask when interviewing someone about their idea. Ask one question at a time and always offer what a strong answer sounds like.

---

## Reason to exist

### 1. Find the workaround

**Claim.** The strongest signal that people want something is that they are already going through a distorted, clumsy process to get it.

**Why.** Adoption of a product that crystallizes an existing chase is intense, not incremental, because you are removing friction from a behavior instead of creating one. Latent demand is also the only demand you can observe before building.

**Story.** The top app in the US store was an anonymous-messaging app written entirely in Arabic, while teens were separately running an emoji game on their stories to fish for compliments. Both were the same desire, to hear good things about yourself, pursued through terrible tools. A compliments-only app built on that desire saw one school send 450,000 messages in its first week; sixty messages per user on day one, where a normal messaging app is lucky to see four. Stripping the harm out of the motive made volume go up, not down.

In a crowded B2B category the same signal is emotional: everyone's competitor already asked about jobs to be done, so the open ground is the moments in a user's week where they feel bad without naming it. "I hate Mondays" turned out to mean gathering data for a Monday report that a button could produce.

**Trap.** Inventing a value people ought to want, then trying to educate them into wanting it.

**Ask.**
- What are people already doing, in some clumsy way, to get the value you're offering? Show me the workaround. *(Strong answer: a specific behavior you have watched, with a tool it abuses.)*
- Where in their day or week does your user feel bad about this, and what do they do next? *(Strong: a moment, a feeling, a coping ritual.)*

### 2. Answer "why this instead of what came before"

**Claim.** A product only earns adoption when it has an answer to why someone would use it instead of what they use today, and that answer has to survive perfect distribution.

**Why.** Feature parity plus a new coat of paint is a working product that fails. The medium changed; a product that merely digitizes the previous artifact leaves the reason to switch on the table. The right move is to take the old category apart and reassemble it around what the new medium can do that the old one could not.

**Story.** A local-search product was a print directory grafted onto a search engine. It got a link on the most visited homepage in the world, the most distribution anyone could give it, and still underperformed, because the link exposed the missing reason to exist instead of hiding it. The successor made the ugly licensed map thumbnail the whole canvas and merged three separate categories (maps, local search, directions) into one; ten million users on day one. Satellite imagery was not the core value but it was the sizzle that pulled ninety million people in on a single day to look at their own roofs. Why people first try and why they stay are different, and you need both.

**Trap.** Believing distribution or a better feature list will make up for the absence of a reason to switch.

**Ask.**
- Why would someone use this instead of what they use today, and would that answer still hold if you had the best distribution in the world? *(Strong: a thing the old way cannot do, not a thing yours does better.)*
- Is this a digital version of the old thing, or has the category been rebuilt around what the new technology makes possible? *(Strong: a hierarchy you inverted.)*
- What is the sizzle that gets someone to try it, and what is the enduring value that keeps them, and are you confusing the two?

### 3. Say the real motive out loud

**Claim.** Every product serves a primal motive or sells a practice, and a team that will not name it cannot iterate toward it.

**Why.** People download consumer apps for three reasons: to make or save money, to find a mate, or to unplug from reality. Free texting, dating, and games. "An app for teens to flirt" is unpresentable in a corporate review, which is exactly why big companies cannot build it and exactly why being honest about it is a startup's edge. In B2B, adopting a tool means adopting a practice; the tool's defaults become the buyer's floor of competence. "Opinionated" is only worth anything when the opinion encodes what high-performing teams already agree on, packaged so a team not doing it yet can switch it on.

**Story.** An issue tracker watched several companies run triage well by hand, automated exactly that, and gave everyone else a button to adopt the practice. Two opinions are just two opinions; consensus is a product.

**Trap.** Dressing the motive up in mission language until the team forgets what the product is for.

**Ask.**
- Which of the three (money, mate, unplug) is this really serving, and can you say it without dressing it up? *(Strong: one word, no qualifier.)*
- What practice does a team adopt when they adopt your tool, and is it a consensus practice among great teams or just your preference?

### 4. Build for a named person with a specific bad feeling

**Claim.** Tie every product debate to a real person with a first name, a last name, and an email, and to the specific moment that made them feel bad.

**Why.** Reality is ugly. A beautiful solution that does not match how a real person actually works gets admired and then not used. Personas launder that ugliness away. The feeling matters because five-whys and outcome framing miss the emotional charge that motivates the request, and you only reach it in a conversation long enough that the person starts leveling with you.

**Story.** A product manager set a December 30 ship date because the project was a Q4 project. Marketing lost their minds over a holiday launch. The PM swore never to put a date on anything again. The product answer was not "dates on or off" but target dates at any granularity, month, quarter, half, so nobody promises false precision again. Three different bad feelings (drowning in manual routing, everything feels out of control, nobody answers my tickets) turned out to share one root, and one rotation-based triage queue fixed all three.

Keep the loop unfiltered: put live chat in the product and let users tell you their problem in their own words; route the angry issues and replies for a feature to the person who built it, never through a summarizer. Product sense is years of someone yelling at you when you got it wrong.

**Trap.** Personas, aggregated feedback, and anyone on the team who is insulated from the consequences of what they shipped.

**Ask.**
- Who, by name and email, has this problem today, and how exactly do they work around it? *(Strong: you can message them right now.)*
- What specific moment made them feel bad, and what did they promise themselves they would never do again? *(Strong: a story with a date in it.)*
- Who on your team receives the complaints about this, and is it the person who built it?

### 5. Treat what customers say as a signal, not a finding

**Claim.** What customers and users say is rarely the literal truth, and the founder's story about why they said no is one of the most dangerous objects in a company.

**Why.** People are social animals; they soften rejection the way a breakup is "it's not you, it's me." A lost deal comes back as "too expensive" when the buyer saw little value. Repeat that to the team twice and it becomes a fact, and the company goes into a pricing discussion instead of confronting differentiation. Founders are also single-issue voters for their own skill: engineers see an engineering problem, designers see a redesign, sellers see a partnership. At least a third of the time the chosen fix was chosen for comfort.

**Story.** A social product with eleven engineers out of twelve people, more features, and perfect uptime lost to a competitor whose site went down daily, in the summer three celebrities joined the competitor. It lost for no reason related to product at all, and nobody inside had asked an outsider what they were missing. Confidence is not correlated with the quality of advice; ask why until you can reconstruct the framework and apply it yourself.

**Trap.** A comfortable explanation, repeated until it is load-bearing.

**Ask.**
- When a customer said no, what did they literally say, and what do you think the real reason was? *(Strong: the quote, then a reason that is not flattering to you.)*
- Is the fix you are proposing the thing you happen to be best at? What would a founder with the opposite background do?
- Which outsider has told you something about this idea you did not want to hear?

## What to build

### 6. Your product has one good idea. Count the steps to it.

**Claim.** Most products have one good idea, and the whole job is getting a new user to experience it as fast as possible.

**Why.** Products accumulate steps between hearing about them and feeling them, by accident and constantly. Attention on mobile is about three seconds; every tap is a miracle and users bounce between apps at high frequency. Activation is a tap-count problem, and great product people know every platform mechanism that collapses taps.

**Story.** A shopping tool had a buried feature that found the cheapest version of any product page. Instead of surfacing it in the app, the team bought a short domain and taught people to type it in front of any URL. Millions in revenue in under sixty days. Exchanging usernames is ten thousand taps; a one-tap contact sync that ranks friends already on the app does the same job. One founder keeps a command that spins up a fresh install in a clean container and runs it every two weeks; he always finds something the team broke by accident.

**Trap.** Onboarding that asks for setup before it has shown value; a first session designed by people who have not been new users in a year.

**Ask.**
- What is the one good idea, and how many steps stand between someone hearing about it and experiencing it? *(Strong: one sentence, and a number under five.)*
- Does a new user see value within three seconds of the first screen? What do they see instead?
- When did you last go through a fresh install as a brand-new user, and what broke?

### 7. Absorb fifty problems, ship one solution

**Claim.** The job of product is not to receive a problem and ship the immediate fix; it is to absorb many problems and find the one solution that dissolves fifty of them.

**Why.** Responding one-to-one to every request, complaint, and competitor feature maps neatly onto "prompt the agent" and feels like shipping a thousand features. It adds up to a horrible product with nothing cohesive in it. When many people ask for a big general feature, the general feature is the road to bloat; inside the requests is a concentrated pool of people who mean the same thing, and that thing can be solved natively.

**Story.** Forty percent of the requests for custom fields on an issue tracker were people wanting to record that a specific customer had asked for something. The team never built custom fields; it built customer requests, auto-linked from support and CRM tools, so the engineer does nothing manually and can read the actual customer email while building. Managers got their report; engineers got more context and zero extra work. A product is an abstraction over many issues, and building that abstraction is a skill you improve at forever.

**Trap.** A roadmap that is a list of inputs.

**Ask.**
- What single solution absorbs fifty of your users' problems, rather than one feature per complaint? *(Strong: a mechanism, not a feature.)*
- Inside the big general feature everyone is asking for, what concentrated pool of use cases could you solve natively instead?

### 8. Think a lot instead of swinging a lot

**Claim.** Pre-fit, the hard part is deciding what to do, and most directions can be eliminated in your head and in conversation. Faster building does not speed that up at all.

**Why.** Being able to ship ten times more does not produce ten times as many good ideas. Everything shipped is supported forever, and every future feature has to interact with it, so more throughput should make a team more conservative, not less. Founders who are honest about it find they are not moving faster than before; it just feels like it, because the typing got faster while the deciding did not.

**Story.** A developer-tools team took swings at several ideas and never launched most of them, because building revealed they made no sense. After finding fit, the founder's main job became slowing everyone down; his memo listed "shipping features not worth shipping" as challenge number one and set out which kinds of things ship now and which wait for clarity. Prepare, set the foundations, then spring; you spring with far more force than if you had forced it early.

**Trap.** Velocity as a proxy for progress.

**Ask.**
- For each thing on the roadmap, how will it interact with everything already shipped, and what will you be supporting forever because of it?
- Are you actually moving faster than competitors, or does it feel that way because someone else is doing the typing?

### 9. Do less, and remove the switch

**Claim.** Do not design it if you do not have to. Extend what exists, build primitives that compose, and treat any mode or toggle as a temporary state.

**Why.** When anyone can make anything, the highest-leverage design move is often finding the existing component to build on or deciding the feature is not needed. Composable primitives are the only way a product with many capabilities feels like one simple thing. A feature reaches everyone only when it no longer requires a switch; most users extract a sliver of what a product can do, and that is fine, so the default stays extremely simple and the power stays reachable for those who want it. Design simultaneously for the novice who does not know what this can do and the daily user you must get out of the way of.

**Story.** An assistant product runs its experiments in a desktop app and a developer tool, with people who care, before anything reaches the main app and its hundreds of millions of users. A separate work mode is treated as a step on the way to being absorbed into the default, not as a destination. An electric truck unlocks as you approach and never needs turning on or off; great design deletes steps people assumed were fundamental.

**Trap.** A settings page as the answer to a disagreement.

**Ask.**
- What existing system or feature could you extend instead, and do you need this feature at all?
- Where is the switch or mode in your design, and what would it take to remove it so it works for everyone by default?
- What does the novice see first, and how do you get out of the way of the person who uses it every day?

### 10. Let the promise pick the extreme

**Claim.** State the one promise the product makes, then find solutions by building the most extreme version along that trait and the extreme in the other direction, and locating where they meet.

**Why.** The biggest decision-making risk is that the right option was never in your set. Extremes break the invisible constraints you carry and expand the search. The promise dictates which end of the spectrum you can live at: "you always have a car" forces surge pricing; "the price is always predictable" means sometimes there is no car. You only find the right amount by overshooting: the correct amount is too much minus one.

**Story.** For issue drafts, the fastest version (closing discards, no prompt) felt unsafe internally. The safest version (autosave every keystroke) shipped and left a trail of untitled drafts. The final design interrupts on a brand-new issue and silently autosaves an existing draft. The team's promise is speed, so the first experiment was the fastest possible one.

**Trap.** Choosing between two moderate options that were both wrong.

**Ask.**
- What is the one promise your product makes? *(Strong: a sentence a user would repeat.)*
- What is the most extreme version of a solution along that trait? Have you built it and felt it?

### 11. Refuse the requests that trade the user's day for the buyer's report

**Claim.** One category of request gets an automatic no: customization asked for by the person who buys, to make their reporting easier at the cost of the workflow of the person who uses the product all day.

**Why.** This is precisely what makes incumbents bloated and hated. The daily user is paid to do their job, not to fill in fields, so they disengage; the data goes sparse and wrong; the report the buyer wanted is garbage anyway. The sales pressure that says the deal dies without this feature is a false trade-off: buyers are buying team effectiveness, and when you show them these customizations are why their current setup is a mess, they stop wanting them. Of a laundry list of ten, three actually decide the deal. Solve those far better than anyone. Do the keep-the-lights-on scaling work (SSO, provisioning) and refuse anything that touches the product's business logic.

**Story.** A required dropdown with nine options the engineer does not understand, so they always pick the first one. Enterprise adoption turned out to be an identity decision, "is this for us", that followed brand, and "no" mostly meant "not now": companies that declined came back two years later.

**Trap.** "Focus on the user" as if the user were one person; find where incentives inside your user base are misaligned and decide, looking for the win-win where it exists.

**Ask.**
- Which requests will you refuse categorically, and does the answer protect the daily user from the buyer's wishes? *(Strong: a named category and a reason.)*
- Where do the incentives of the person who buys and the person who uses diverge, and which side wins when they do?
- Of the ten things prospects say they need, which three decide the deal?

### 12. Design the system, not the mockup

**Claim.** The product has to produce a good experience from input you do not control; the comp with perfect photos and grammatical posts is the easy part.

**Why.** Real content is ugly, uneven, and hostile. A design reviewed only with ideal data will look like garbage the moment real data hits it. Systems thinking, knowing what is hard, easy, possible, and impossible when the system runs at scale, is the hardest part of building products and matters more as code gets cheaper to generate. The bottleneck was never the prototype; it is maintaining and changing a complex system without breaking it.

**Story.** A feed team required every design to be presented with real, messy data after the first mockups looked beautiful and the live product did not. A "like" button was invented because seventy percent of comments were one-word acknowledgments crowding out real discussion; the heart was rejected because it failed on tragic posts, so the icon was made as neutral as the range of content demanded.

**Trap.** Falling in love with the comp.

**Ask.**
- What does this look like with real, ugly, uncontrolled data instead of your mockup?
- Which feature exists because of a specific product problem you observed, and which exists because it looked good in the design?

## How to test it

### 13. Write the chain of must-be-trues, four links at most

**Claim.** Write the product as a chain of "if this is true, then what must be true next", validate in that order, and execute at 100% only on the link under test.

**Why.** Each conditional layer is a place the product can fail, so more links means a riskier product. Trying to validate everything at once produces scope creep and a muddy signal. Building the layer under test perfectly and half-building the rest is what makes the result interpretable.

**Story.** A polling app validated in strict sequence: will people use the core polling flow, will it spread inside one school, will it hop schools, will people pay. The polling experience (questions, notifications) was made perfect before sharing or virality were touched. Whether a consumer idea works is unpredictable, so a reproducible testing process raises your odds more than any single idea; the first app took a year to build, the fifteenth took two weeks.

**Trap.** Trying to prove the whole idea in one launch.

**Ask.**
- What must be true for this to work, in order? Give me the four links and tell me which one you are testing right now. *(Strong: four sentences, one marked.)*
- For the link you are testing this month, what is executed at 100% and what are you deliberately half-building?

### 14. Engineer out every excuse before the test

**Claim.** Design every test so a failure cannot be blamed on execution. If you can walk away saying "maybe we didn't do it right", it was not a test.

**Why.** Mobilizing a team to test is expensive, so a test has to return a clean yes or no. If a social product needs friends to work, force enough density by hand that "they didn't have enough friends" is never available as the excuse. Manual seeding, personal onboarding, and 24-hour live support are how you test, not how you grow. The seed gets you the first hundred users and the density the network needs; after that the product has to grow on its own, or it did not work.

**Story.** A team got an entire school to adopt on the same day by geo-targeted ads and following students from a dedicated account, so they could see whether everyone having ten friends produced value. Founders who later copied the school-by-school tactic at fifteen schools and wondered why it stopped working had confused the test with the growth. Rerunning the same concept five years later at the same school on the same day produced messages but no growth, because the growth climate had changed and every layer had to be re-earned.

**Trap.** Leaving yourself an out.

**Ask.**
- If this test fails, what excuse will you have left? What confounding variable have you not eliminated? *(Strong: "none, and here is how".)*
- Once you stop pushing by hand, what has to happen for the product to grow on its own, and how will you know it did not?

### 15. Something workable at 10% of the time, released through widening circles

**Claim.** By the time 10% of the time budget has elapsed there should be a working version that tests a key hypothesis, declared in advance to be not great, and every feature ships through expanding circles of users.

**Why.** If you learn at 80% that an assumption was wrong, it is too late: deadlines slip and the launch window is gone. Declaring the first version will not be great is what makes it cheap; nobody sands anything down, you skip pixel-perfect design and just get software you can feel. Each circle (the team, then early access, then beta, then everyone) is a test, and if nobody in early access engages you missed. Speed here requires engineers who will build something workable even when they disagree with a design choice. Match the depth of process to how hardened the thing is: some features get a hundred tries and one ship, others go from idea to live in four hours in public.

**Story.** A team sets a milestone of "this is the demo we want to show people" and pushes to it as fast as possible; once a foundation exists and people roughly get it, vocal users tell you what else it needs, where it has to work, and what sucks. Getting to the demo is the painful uphill part. One design leader prototypes ideas at night with an agent so there is an artifact to criticize in the morning; another builds weekend prototypes with no obligation to ship, purely so colleagues can react.

**Trap.** Estimating instead of shipping early, and polishing a version nobody has used.

**Ask.**
- If this takes three months, what is working and testable internally by the end of week one, and what assumption does it test?
- What are your circles, and what would "nobody in early access touched it" make you do?
- Can you put a version in front of people in a day? If not, why does this one deserve the slow path?

### 16. Fit is binary; if you are asking, you do not have it

**Claim.** When a product is working you know: things break every few days, you have to invent new metrics, and people are fighting to get in. Any uncertainty is a no.

**Why.** Working products force operational chaos that cannot be faked. And the inverse: a project can run for two years with a happy status report and no fit. Interrogate whether the thing you are on matters regardless of what the reports say, because the users it never had will not tell you. When it does work, throttle deliberately and put out the largest fires first; everything you built needs replacing every few days during breakout, and controlled rollout buys the slack to stay online.

**Story.** A social app had to be geo-fenced state by state to keep servers up; its support system broke after three days and the replacement seven days later; the team's metric became hourly actives because daily actives stopped meaning anything. Turning off something that is working was controversial internally, but it re-ignited on relaunch. Separately, a two-year technical project with hundreds of users was cancelled by a visiting executive and people were surprised; nobody had asked whether it had the right users or any feedback loop at all.

**Trap.** Squinting at a dashboard for fit.

**Ask.**
- What broke last week because too many people used this? *(Strong: a specific outage or a metric you had to invent.)*
- What evidence, other than the status report, says this project matters?

## How it wins

### 17. The better product loses to distribution, and positioning decides who helps you

**Claim.** You can have the better product and lose. Decide who brings the users or the supply, choose the go-to-market from how the product is actually bought, and claim the territory that makes larger players want you to win.

**Why.** A follower network needs people worth following; polishing features while a competitor seeds supply is losing for reasons unrelated to product. Go-to-market starts with one question: are the user and the buyer the same person? If yes, product-led growth works; if not, you need direct sales, and a growth motion that never touches the buyer will not grow. Developer tools are consumer products adopted bottom-up by individuals who carry them into companies, and programmers are bad at consumer products. Positioning is the highest-leverage decision available to a small team: hold the neutral ground nobody claimed and billion-dollar companies advance their own interests by helping you. Growth systems must be above board, because the internet is an organism that gets even.

**Story.** A coding tool looked at a crowded market, saw nobody held "open source, works with every model", and took it. When one provider cut it off at nine at night, a competitor announced official support the next morning. Its core was mediocre for five months, but its first impression and frictionless onboarding won share, and once it had share it went back and made the core good. It removed an onboarding blocker by building a service it never planned, which became a business of its own. Name and icon turned out to be conversion surfaces at the moment of invite: one name half the users would not send killed the loop; a rename lifted the invite rate immediately. Marketing and in-product growth are one system and must speak to the same community end to end.

**Trap.** "If we build it better, they will come."

**Ask.**
- Who brings the supply, the users, or the buyers, and what is your plan for that beyond making the product better? *(Strong: a named channel and a first hundred.)*
- Who uses this and who signs the check, and does your motion reach the person with the budget?
- Which territory in this market is unclaimed, and who benefits from a neutral party holding it?
- Is this adopted bottom-up by individuals? Then what does it feel like in the first ten seconds compared to the incumbent?
- Does any part of your growth loop use user data in a way you would be uncomfortable showing the user?

### 18. Quality comes from irrational investments, and speed is not its opposite

**Claim.** About half of what a quality-focused team does it did not strictly have to do, and that half is what users notice first. Speed and quality are not a trade-off; speed is a symptom of competence.

**Why.** Hyper-rational logic can build a business, but it will not produce a product people feel is great. Most great products could be half as good with no measurable impact, and once "nothing has to be good" gets into your head you stop shipping good things, because laziness in one place spreads to every place like an infection. Quality comes from iterations, iterations require speed, and at the top of any craft you can tell how good the output will be by how fast the person works. Products now rot within a year, so quality is one of the few differentiators left to a small team.

**Story.** A terminal tool built its own rendering framework, the first thing every engineering team is told not to do, and the early reason people switched to it was that the incumbent flickered and felt janky. Even users it was not for walked away thinking the people who built it were competent. Skip growth hacks that are obvious to the user: a commit-message watermark was turned off by default the moment users asked for a toggle, because everyone could see why it was there.

**Trap.** "Users won't notice" as a decision rule.

**Ask.**
- Which irrational quality investment are you making that a rational team would skip, and is it the thing users notice first?
- Do you believe this product has to be good, or do you privately think quality does not matter because sloppy products still make money?

### 19. Technical moats are comically narrow; product moats last

**Claim.** What is R&D today is a weekend task in three years. Build what the technology cannot yet do knowing you will throw it away, be precious only about what has hardened, and know what happens the day the platform ships your feature.

**Why.** Putting a database in the cloud was a technical moat in 1998 and is trivial now; the product moat stayed. A giant workaround for a platform flaw means you are building the wrong thing: for every gap, decide explicitly whether it is yours to fix or whether to wait for the platform, and sometimes just wait. The scaffolding built to get value now gets discarded when the platform catches up, and the conversation moves from technology to product, which is where you have to be able to win. Do not be precious about a design until the technology under it has stopped moving; polish only what will not be invalidated next month. Separate the durable core from the interface and enforce the boundary structurally, so the inside can change freely. "I could build this in a weekend" always gets said about relevant products; the value sits under the forms, in the relationships, the trust, and the social proof that lets a buyer be fifth instead of first.

**Story.** A support-automation company discarded its home-built reasoning scaffolding the month a better model shipped, and expects its best-in-market voice work to be commoditized within three years; the bet is that the same clients stay for the product. An agent team treats every helper it adds as a crutch the next model should make unnecessary, and warns new joiners about the ten-thousand-line crutch. Coupling the harness to one model was rejected because anyone could fork it and change ten lines, and refusing optionality is a decision you cannot explain as correct.

**Trap.** Calling a temporary technical lead a moat, and polishing something the next platform release will delete.

**Ask.**
- Which part of what you built will be trivial in three years, and what is the product moat that survives that? *(Strong: names the disposable part without flinching.)*
- If the platform or model provider ships your feature at their next event, why will customers keep choosing you?
- Which parts are hardened enough to obsess over, and which will the next release make obsolete?
- When someone says they could build it in a weekend, what is under the surface they are missing?

### 20. Sell a measurable outcome, and price on it if you can

**Claim.** A job completed autonomously has self-evident, countable value; unattributable productivity is nearly impossible to sell. Usage does not correlate with value, so price as close to the outcome as the product allows.

**Why.** "Every salesperson sells five percent more, pay us a million" is a conversation nobody wins. A long interaction that fails to solve the problem has negative value, so paying per unit of usage is like measuring engineers by lines of code. Outcome pricing forces the vendor to own the last mile and become a partner who has to make the customer succeed; escalations become free and efficiency becomes the vendor's problem. Where the outcome is not yet measurable, define it anyway and keep the company spring-loaded toward it. Narrow the domain until the problem turns from science into engineering; the unit to automate is an end-to-end process, not a person or a department, and someone at the customer should own it with a KPI. Your top support request is your monetization roadmap.

**Story.** A support agent is paid a pre-negotiated rate per resolved conversation against a human cost of ten to twenty dollars a call; some sales agents are paid a commission. A compliments app's most common support message was "can I pay to reveal who sent this?" That unanswered question became the paid feature of its successor, roughly ten million dollars in sales on ten million downloads, with no investors. A hundredfold cost collapse did not just save money; a retailer's conversation volume went up two to three times, and the CEO was happy because they were finally listening to their clients.

**Trap.** Selling a feeling of productivity, and pricing on the thing that is easy to meter.

**Ask.**
- Does this complete a job the customer can measure, or are you selling productivity nobody can attribute? *(Strong: a number the customer already tracks.)*
- Could you get paid only when the outcome happens? If not, what would have to change about the product?
- What is the most common message in your support inbox, and what does it say people would pay for?
- If you cut the cost of this interaction a hundredfold, what would customers start doing that they could not afford before?
